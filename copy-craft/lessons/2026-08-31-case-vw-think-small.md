# Case note: Think Small named the disadvantage.

**Source:** Craft notes, not a reprint. Wikipedia "Think Small" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think_Small, fetched 2026-08-31); Brandvelle campaign recap (https://brandvelle.com/articles/volkswagen-think-small-ddb-beetle, fetched 2026-08-31). DDB, 1959. Art: Helmut Krone. Copy: Julian Koenig. Bernbach oversaw.
**Date added:** 2026-08-31

**Principle:**
The move: in a market shouting chrome and size, they photographed the car small on a lot of white, named the supposed flaw in two words, then paid it off with quiet engineering facts. Confession, then proof. Layout did half the selling.

**Popsmith failure mode:**
Copying white space and self-deprecation ("it's just popcorn") as if understatement were a style you can borrow. VW was selling against Detroit bloat. We are selling a $199 object she is proud to leave out. Naming a fake flaw ("expensive unitasker") only works if the payoff is heirloom, and only if a customer already said it.

**One line we might run:**
Yes, it is an expensive unitasker. My grandchildren will fight over it one day.

**Where this fails on Popsmith:**
A kitchen appliance brand cannot "think small" the product. The Popper's job is to look like it belongs next to Le Creuset, not to disappear on the page. Steal the honesty, not the layout.
