I think it's because I processed the world through food. You know, I was always like this. Even when I was a toddler, I used to climb my grandmother's pantry in the kitchen and look for the spiciest pickles and they would keep moving these pickle jars up and up and up, 'cause they were worried that my little stomach at, you know, two and three and four was going to be affected badly by all the spicy food. I mean, they didn't know what to do. And at a certain point they just left me alone because it wasn't hurting me.

Then a few years ago, only when I was filming "Top Chef" in Seattle, I went to the Science Center and I met this Italian researcher who did a test on me, and she said, "Oh, you are a super taster." I always thought that this was bullshit, you know, I didn't realize that it was an actual thing. And she said, "Yes, the way we do it is by a totally blind test. You can take it now." Like if you read Proust, you know, you bite a mandarin and you're immediately transported back to his childhood, right? And so it's the same thing for me.

I mean, I had two completely different upbringings, both equally important influences. You know, my life in America with my mom in New York City was exciting. I had a lot of autonomy. I was a latchkey kid. And in India we lived eight to 10 people in a little two bedroom apartment. My grandmother cooked for that many people on a two burner stove. She never used the fridge. My grandmother never got a fridge until 1977. And so she didn't really know what to do with the fridge except for keep ice water in it, you know? So she was cooking constantly.

And I had all of these influences. My grandparents, of course, my grandfather loved books, which is why I think I'm a writer and I love to read. And my grandmother was a great cook. And through teaching me how to cook, my grandmother also taught me, you know, about life. She was a very practical woman. She was not a cuddly grandma. You know, she didn't have the luxury of time to be one. And so she would always teach me things. like she would say, you know, we would make like a stew of some kind. She would say, "Don't put everything in at once. There's a right time for everything. Everything cooks at a different timetable. And it's your job to know how to add things in what order. And it's the same thing in your life. You want so much independence, but you're not ready for that independence. And if I give you independence too early, it's going to make you, in a way, distorted, because you're not ready to handle that. And I want you to have a good experience for all the things that come your way." Just like that, soup has to be ready. You know, for the cilantro, only put the cilantro at the end. Potatoes much earlier, cilantro at the end. So, you know, in teaching me all these principles of food, as a young girl, I'm talking 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, she also taught me a lot about life.

And in my grandmother's kitchen, there was a very big hierarchy, just like a professional kitchen, you know, first you were only allowed to break the ends off beans or wash the vegetables. Then when you got, you know, in sixth grade, then you could start chopping. Then when you were a teenager, you were finally allowed to turn on the stove and make tea. But you were never allowed to do anything. Not even make the rice until you were in college. And then before you got married, you were taught what you needed to know.
